Meeting Summary — 19 August 2026
1. Purpose of the meeting
The meeting was primarily to define the group CAS project's direction and broad structure, particularly what would replace/evolve the previous coding challenge.
The discussion began with the original idea of running a traditional competitive-programming competition, but the group concluded that this format is less suitable today because AI can easily solve many algorithmic problems, policing AI use would be difficult, and fewer students may be interested in pure competitive programming. The proposed alternative became an AI-enabled, social-impact hackathon, supported by a broader technology-literacy initiative.
The project therefore evolved into two connected components:
Literacy / education programme — workshops for NGOs, students and potentially other groups.
Hackathon — the final culminating event, where participants create technology-based solutions to social problems.

2. Main Topics Discussed
A. Competitive programming vs. an AI hackathon
The original competition was based on the school's previous coding challenge: several algorithmic problems of increasing difficulty solved in languages such as Python/C++. The group felt this had several problems:
AI makes enforcing a no-AI rule difficult.
Students could simply use AI and then dispute disqualifications.
Most of the organising team does not currently have strong competitive-programming experience.
A build-oriented hackathon was expected to attract more students.
A hackathon makes it much easier to connect the project to social problems and SDGs.
Direction chosen: move away from a traditional coding challenge and toward an AI-permitted hackathon focused on solving social problems.

B. Overall project structure: literacy + hackathon
A significant part of the meeting was spent deciding whether the project should be more than just a competition.
The group broadly settled on:
Part 1 — Literacy programme
Run educational sessions before the hackathon.
Some can be offline sessions, particularly with NGOs/community groups.
Some can be online preparation sessions for hackathon participants.
Part 2 — Hackathon
Remains the project's main/final event.
All members of the group should participate in running the final hackathon, even if different people lead different literacy workshops beforehand.
This was viewed as a way of making the project more educational and socially meaningful rather than simply organising a competition.

C. Literacy programme topics
Several possible literacy areas were discussed. The main working set was:
AI literacy
Basic coding / software / vibe coding
Cybersecurity, potentially connected with AI
Digital financial literacy, including topics such as fraud, internet banking, investments/portfolio management and more
Hardware/electronics/engineering
Presentation, pitching and solution-development skills
There was concern about adding too many disconnected topics simply because different members wanted something aligned with their own interests. The group therefore agreed that the topics eventually need to be limited and clearly defined.
A useful distinction emerged:
AI/vibe-coding and pitching workshops can directly prepare people for the hackathon.
Digital finance, cybersecurity and potentially hardware can form part of the wider offline literacy effort.
The transcript suggests an expectation of approximately 6–8 literacy sessions, although this was an estimate rather than a fixed commitment.

D. NGO/community component
The group discussed taking literacy sessions to NGOs or other community groups.
Potential implementation included:
running sessions at multiple centres;
possibly working with organisations that operate several locations;
teaching basic coding/technology concepts before introducing more advanced subjects;
adapting the material to the audience rather than assuming prior technical knowledge.
For this part, the group recognised that they would need:
school/CAS approval;
approval from the NGO/community organisation;
possibly permission letters or other documentation;
suitable audiences for each workshop topic.
Aryan and Prathmesh were particularly associated with planning and executing the offline literacy/NGO side, with others able to join them.
Hardware remained less certain because running hands-on electronics sessions would require equipment, materials and possibly devices for participants.

3. Hackathon Structure Discussed
Core concept
The hackathon should be solution-focused rather than purely coding-focused.
Teams would broadly:
Receive a theme / social problem area.
Identify or work within a defined problem.
Develop a proposed solution.
Build some form of minimum viable product (MVP).
Prepare a presentation/deck.
Pitch the solution to judges.
The group explicitly leaned toward evaluating the quality and feasibility of the overall solution rather than simply the sophistication of the code. Implementation thinking and the pitch therefore matter alongside the technical prototype.
The expected deliverable was described as roughly:
MVP + pitch/deck + explanation of how the solution would actually be implemented.

Theme / problem statements
The broad direction was to use social challenges / SDGs as the underlying theme.
However, different levels may receive different amounts of guidance:
Younger category: more specific problem areas/questions should be provided so participants do not simply ask AI for generic ideas.
Older category: can receive a broader social-impact/SDG theme and have greater freedom to define their own problem and solution.
The exact prompts were not finalised during this meeting.

Age categories
The working structure became two divisions, approximately:
Junior: Grade 10 and below — discussion varied between Grades 6–10 and 8–10.
Senior: Grade 11 and above, with discussion about potentially allowing college students.
The important agreement was that younger students should not compete directly against much older participants.
The precise lower-grade boundary still needs confirmation.

Team size
After substantial discussion, the clearest final decision was:
Maximum 4 participants per team, with solo participation potentially permitted.
The transcript explicitly ends this discussion with: “Max 4 people in a team. Okay, done. That's confirmed.”

Online vs. offline
Both formats were debated.
Offline advantages included interaction and easier collaboration, but disadvantages included:
venue cost;
food;
charging/equipment requirements;
expecting students and organisers to remain physically present for many hours.
The meeting ultimately leaned strongly toward an online hackathon, similar to the previous coding challenge.
Discord emerged as the likely platform because it could handle:
announcements;
team communication;
voice channels;
participant questions;
continuous organiser support/"office hours".

Hackathon flow
The proposed structure was roughly:
Opening ceremony → guest speaker → challenge/theme explanation → Q&A → build period → submission → judging/pitches.
During the build period, organisers could remain available on Discord in rotating shifts so participants can ask questions.
A participant rulebook/information packet should also explain:
themes/problem areas;
possible directions;
examples;
rules;
submission requirements.

Duration and tentative date
Different options from 6–8 hours through 24 hours were debated. A 24-hour online format received significant support but was not completely locked.
The working tentative date became:
24–25 October 2026, ideally beginning Saturday and concluding Sunday.
The group also recognised that literacy workshops would consequently have to start considerably earlier, likely in September, with some sessions occurring before October.

4. Funding, Prizes and Sponsorship
These were discussed extensively but not finalised.
Ideas included:
participant registration fees;
cash prizes;
non-cash prizes/subscriptions;
donating profits/remainder to NGOs;
external sponsors.
A working example used ₹2,000 per team, but the meeting repeatedly returned to the point that the precise fee and prize structure should be dealt with later.
The strongest general principle was:
Some proceeds could support prizes, while remaining profits could be donated to NGOs/community organisations connected with the literacy programme.
External sponsorship is possible, but school approval/disclosure would have to be checked before approaching sponsors formally.

5. Key Takeaways / Decisions
The major outcome of the meeting was that the project is no longer being conceived as simply a coding competition. It is now intended as a larger technology-literacy + social-impact hackathon project.
The strongest working agreements were:
Build an AI-enabled hackathon, rather than banning AI.
Focus the hackathon on social problems / SDGs.
Make the hackathon the final culminating event.
Add a literacy programme beforehand.
Include AI/software education, digital finance/cybersecurity and potentially hardware/electronics within that programme.
Run separate online hackathon-preparation workshops.
Judge teams primarily on the solution, implementation thinking, MVP and presentation, rather than code alone.
Use two broad age divisions.
Give younger students more structured problem statements.
Set 4 participants as the maximum team size.
Strongly favour an online hackathon, probably run through Discord.
All project members participate in the final hackathon, while preparatory work is divided among smaller groups.
Tentatively target 24–25 October 2026 for the final event.
Final details around prizes, exact prompts, judging rubric, sponsors and fee structure remain open.
